01 / Technical / System Investigation

All roads lead
to Rome.

A contact-level eligibility rule collided with user-level campaign orchestration. The problem wasn't the logic—it was where the platform was evaluating it.

System Investigation Solution Design Technical Validation

Challenge

A contact-level eligibility rule collided with user-level campaign orchestration, leaving a critical campaign requirement without a supported path forward. With the issue blocking delivery and escalating client concern, a workable solution had become urgent.

What I did

I reproduced the behavior, traced the platform boundary, and worked with Engineering to confirm the constraint. With conventional options exhausted, I designed a workaround that Engineering validated as a viable path forward. I kept investigating, and a final alternative uncovered a simpler supported solution that avoided the workaround entirely.

Outcome

The final approach evaluated eligibility in the recipient context during message generation—putting the rule where the required contact information was actually available and removing a critical blocker without relying on the more complex fallback.

02 / Solution Architecture

Let me count
the ways.

Dozens of brands, campaign tracks, touches, languages, and delivery methods created more than 100 possible outputs. The challenge wasn't creating them—it was creating a system that could manage them.

Solution Architecture System Design Technical Implementation

Challenge

A portfolio of active, revenue-generating campaigns had to be rebuilt on our ESP against a fixed go-to-market deadline. Across dozens of brands, tracks, touches, and languages, the architecture had to support hundreds of campaign permutations—each moving through development, internal QA, and client UAT before it could be pushed to production. The migration window left little room for rework or delay.

What I did

As lead technical designer and developer, I translated that matrix of campaign permutations into an architecture the team could actually build and operate under the deadline. I designed reusable structures across brands and campaign paths, accounted for multiple languages and implementation requirements, and built proofing and QA into the system so complexity could be validated at scale.

Outcome

The campaigns were ready for go-to-market. Instead of treating hundreds of permutations as individual builds, the architecture created a scalable, maintainable system that could be tested and operated consistently across the campaign ecosystem. Following the migration, the client took ownership of ongoing maintenance—without needing the original development team to manage day-to-day changes.

03 / System Design / Enablement

I say tomato.
You say tomahto.

Supporting more than 20 languages wasn't really a translation problem. It was a systems problem: how do you make that much variation reusable, reliable, and operable by the people who actually own the content?

System Design Dynamic Content Client Enablement

Challenge

A multilingual campaign program needed to support more than 20 languages without turning every locale into its own implementation. Content, configuration, and reusable components had to work together consistently—while remaining understandable and maintainable for the client team responsible for operating it.

What I did

I designed and evolved a reusable campaign system that separated language-specific content from the underlying implementation, using locale-driven feeds and shared components to manage variation at scale. When event-driven campaigns exposed a limitation in the original profile-locale model, I adapted the architecture rather than forcing the new use case through an assumption that no longer held.

Outcome

What began as a multilingual implementation became a system the client could operate independently. New content and language variations could be managed through the established structure instead of requiring one-off development, while the architecture remained flexible enough to accommodate campaign models it wasn't originally designed to support.

More Work

Problem

A finalist RFP called for more than a conceptual solution. The proposed campaign experience depended on profile data, lookup data, content feeds, and dynamic content working together—and the team needed to demonstrate that the approach was technically viable.

Approach

I built the technical proof behind the proposed solution, connecting the required data sources and dynamic content logic into a working implementation. I also supported the Solutions Consulting team with the technical detail needed to explain how the concept would work in practice.

Result

The team entered the finalist presentation with a working proof instead of a theoretical promise. The prototype demonstrated that the proposed architecture could support the experience being presented and gave the team concrete technical evidence to stand behind the solution.

Problem

Our internal design team was helping a client establish an email design system, including aesthetic best practices, ADA considerations, and modules expected to be difficult to implement. Before those patterns became standards, the client needed to understand what the most complex modules would mean in production.

Approach

I worked with the design team to identify and confirm the highest-complexity modules, then built working examples for the client. For each, I demonstrated both the industry-standard approach and how I would tackle the problem as an email-development SME, documenting the advantages and tradeoffs of each and providing Litmus rendering results for comparison.

Result

Instead of recommending a single “right” implementation, we gave the client evidence they could use to choose. They could compare rendering behavior, complexity, and maintenance tradeoffs against the capabilities of their own internal team and adopt the approach that best fit how they would actually operate the system.

Problem

A dynamic content implementation relied on source data that wasn't always predictable. Duplicate selections, invalid values, and missing content could all create poor customer experiences if the implementation simply trusted the inputs it received.

Approach

I turned the broader solution into production-ready logic, adding duplicate prevention, defensive validation, and fallback behavior for cases where the source data couldn't produce a valid selection. I tested the implementation across expected and edge-case scenarios before deployment.

Result

Client marketers could independently curate and update content without requiring engineering deployments, while the implementation automatically protected against duplicate or invalid selections. The system degraded gracefully when source data was incomplete, giving the team a dependable editorial workflow that remains in production today.

Approach

Follow the evidence.

The first explanation is rarely the whole story. I trace the system until I understand where the problem actually lives—then solve it there.

Connect the dots.

I separate the pieces, understand how they relate, and give them enough structure to make the whole system easier to operate.

Show your work.

A solution isn't finished when it works for me. It should be understandable, maintainable, scalable, and usable by the people who inherit it.
